Part 1

What is PII Detection?

The problem we're solving and why it matters

Finding Sensitive Info in Text

PII detection is about scanning text and highlighting every piece of personally identifiable information.

My name is John Smith and my SSN is 123-45-6789.
Email me at john@example.com or visit me at
742 Evergreen Terrace, Springfield IL.
Name
SSN
Email
Address
💡
Think of it like a highlighter that automatically marks every sensitive detail in a document — names, IDs, emails, addresses — so you can redact or protect them.

The Two Natures of PII

This is the key insight driving our architecture. PII comes in two flavors:

🔢

Structured PII

Has a predictable format — you can spot it by its shape, not its meaning.

SSN: XXX-XX-XXXX
Credit Card: XXXX-XXXX-XXXX-XXXX
Email: word@word.word
Phone: (XXX) XXX-XXXX
IP Address: XXX.XXX.XXX.XXX
🧠

Soft PII

Requires understanding context — looks like normal words until you read around them.

Name: Jordan Lee
Address: 742 Elm Street
Employer: Acme Corp
Medical condition: diabetes
Political affiliation: Independent
Key Insight
Most models only do one well. We need a model that handles both — pattern matching for structured PII, and language understanding for soft PII.
Part 2

ML Building Blocks

The concepts you need — explained simply

Step 1: Tokens

Before a model can read text, it must be broken into small pieces called tokens.

Tokenization in Action

Input: "John's SSN is 123-45-6789"
John 's SS ##N is 123 - 45 - 67 ##89

Notice: "SSN" becomes two tokens ("SS" + "##N"). The model doesn't see whole words — it sees subword pieces.

💡
Tokens are like Scrabble tiles. Instead of reading whole words, the model reads letter groups. It has a fixed vocabulary of ~50,000 common pieces it knows. Rare words get split into smaller known pieces.

Step 2: Embeddings

Each token gets converted into a list of numbers that captures its meaning.

What's an embedding?

A list of numbers (called a vector) that represents a word's meaning. Similar words get similar numbers.

"king" → [0.21, -0.45, 0.89, ...]
"queen" → [0.19, -0.42, 0.91, ...]
"table" → [-0.71, 0.33, -0.15, ...]

"king" and "queen" have similar numbers because they're related concepts. "table" is very different.

In our model

Each token becomes a vector of 384 numbers. That's the model's "hidden size."

John → [384 numbers]
SSN → [384 numbers]
123 → [384 numbers]
💡
Think of embeddings as GPS coordinates for words. Words that mean similar things are located near each other on a "meaning map." The model navigates this map to understand language.

Step 3: Attention

The core breakthrough that makes modern AI work. It lets the model figure out which words to focus on when understanding each word.

How attention works

"The bank of the river was muddy"

When processing "bank", attention looks at other words. It sees "river" and concludes bank = riverbank, not financial bank. Each word "pays attention" to the words that help explain it.

Attention Heads

One "head" is one perspective. Our model has 6 heads running in parallel — each learns to focus on different relationships:

Head 1 grammar relationships
Head 2 nearby words
Head 3 semantic meaning
Head 4 punctuation patterns
... and so on

Layers (Depth)

Our model has 12 layers stacked. Each layer refines the understanding:

Layers 1-4: basic syntax, word forms
Layers 5-8: phrases, relationships
Layers 9-12: high-level meaning, entities

More layers = deeper understanding, but also slower. 12 is the sweet spot for our size.

💡
Attention is like having 6 team members each reading the same sentence, but each focusing on different clues. Then they compare notes to build a complete understanding.

Step 4: The Encoder

An encoder is the full reading comprehension machine. It takes tokens in, applies attention across many layers, and produces a deep understanding of each token.

Raw Text: "My SSN is 123-45-6789"
↓
Tokenizer → [My] [SS] [##N] [is] [123] [-] [45] [-] [67] [##89]
↓
Embeddings → 384 numbers per token
↓
12 Attention Layers (6 heads each)
↓
Output: 384 numbers per token (now context-aware)
The key difference
Input embeddings are static — "bank" always looks the same. Output embeddings are contextual — "bank" near "river" looks different from "bank" near "money". The encoder adds understanding.

DeBERTa: Our Chosen Encoder

Not all encoders are equal. DeBERTa-v3-xsmall is the one we're using. Here's why.

What makes DeBERTa special?

Disentangled Attention

Regular models mix "what a word means" with "where it is in the sentence" into one signal. DeBERTa keeps them separate, then combines them. This means it can learn that position matters differently for different words.

Smarter Pretraining (RTD)

Instead of guessing missing words (like BERT), DeBERTa plays "spot the fake word" — detecting which words in a sentence have been swapped. This is harder and creates a better learner.

Why the "xsmall" version?

How GLiNER2 Actually Works

To compare fairly, let's first understand the model we're trying to beat.

The GLiNER2 Approach

Multi-Task Input Packing

GLiNER2 prepends special tokens that tell the model what to look for. Every entity type you want detected gets injected into the input:

[P] Classify PII types [SEP]
[E] email [E] phone [E] ssn [E] name [SEP]
[C] sensitive [C] non-sensitive [SEP]
My name is John and my SSN is 123-45...

Then What?

The whole thing — instructions + entity types + your actual text — gets fed through a single big DeBERTa encoder. The model learns to match spans of text to the entity type tokens.

The trade-off
GLiNER2 wins on flexibility — it can handle any entity type and multiple IE tasks. We win on everything PII-specific: speed, size, pattern handling, sequence validity. Since DataFog only needs PII detection, the trade-off is clearly in our favor.

Inference: How Each Model Thinks

The same input, processed two very different ways.

GLiNER2's Approach
Step 1: Pack entity type names into input
→ Input gets longer with every type you add
Step 2: Feed everything through 768-dim encoder
→ 200M params process both instructions and text
Step 3: Match every possible span to every entity type
→ O(n² × types) comparisons — slows with more types
Step 4: Rank and threshold matches
→ No structural guarantee of valid sequences
Bottleneck: More PII types = longer input = slower. GLiNER2 degrades with >30 entity types.
DataFog v1's Approach
Step 1: Tokenize text only (no type packing)
→ Input length is just the text, nothing extra
Step 2a: Character CNN scans for patterns
→ 0.3M params, sub-ms, catches digit/symbol patterns
Step 2b: DeBERTa reads context (in parallel)
→ 22M params, 384-dim, understands meaning
Step 3: Gate fuses both signals per token
→ Automatic character vs. context weighting
Step 4: CRF decodes valid BIO sequence
→ Guaranteed valid output, O(n × K²) — fast
Key advantage: Adding more PII types doesn't slow inference — it's just more output labels, not more input.

Why Smaller Can Be Better

How a 22.7M model can beat a 205M model on PII detection.

🎯

Specialization Beats Generalization

GLiNER2 uses its 205M parameters to learn about every kind of entity and four different tasks. Our 22.7M parameters focus entirely on PII patterns. A sports car beats an SUV on a racetrack.

🔢

Built-in Pattern Detection

GLiNER2 must learn that "123-45-6789" is an SSN purely from training data. Our character CNN gives the model a structural "eye" for digit patterns — it has a head start before training even begins.

⚡

Inductive Bias

The gating mechanism encodes our knowledge that PII has two natures (structured + soft) directly into the architecture. GLiNER2 has no such prior — it must discover this from data alone.

📊

More PII Training Data

We train on 360K PII-specific examples vs. GLiNER2's 254K general IE examples. More relevant data + architecture that knows what to look for = better PII performance.

💡
The kitchen analogy: GLiNER2 is a fully-equipped restaurant kitchen — it can make any dish, but it's expensive and takes up a whole floor. We're building a purpose-built pizza oven. It only makes pizza, but it makes better pizza, faster, in a fraction of the space.
Part 4

Our Architecture

The novel design that makes this possible

The Big Idea: Two Eyes, One Brain

Our model has two separate "eyes" that look at text differently, then a smart fusion layer combines their views.

🔍

Eye 1: Characters

Looks at individual characters and short patterns. Spots things like "XXX-XX-XXXX" or "@" symbols.

Character CNN
📖

Eye 2: Context

Reads the whole sentence to understand meaning. Knows "Jordan" after "my name is" = a name.

DeBERTa Encoder
⚖️

Smart Switch

Learns when to trust each eye. For "123-45-6789" → trust characters. For "Jordan Lee" → trust context.

Gating Fusion
💡
Why this is novel: No published model combines these two approaches for PII detection. General NER models rely on context alone. Regex tools rely on patterns alone. We do both, in one model, with a learned switch.

Component 1: Character CNN

A small, fast neural network that reads individual characters to detect patterns.

How it works

Word: "123-45-6789"
Characters: [1][2][3][-][4][5][-][6][7][8][9]

Filter size 3 scans:
[1,2,3] → "three digits in a row"
[3,-,4] → "digit-dash-digit"
[-,4,5] → "dash then digits"
...slides across all characters

Three filter sizes (3, 4, 5 characters wide) catch patterns at different scales.

What it detects

  • Digit sequences (SSNs, credit cards, phones)
  • Separators between digits (dashes, dots, spaces)
  • @ symbols and dots (emails)
  • Mixed case patterns (camelCase usernames)
  • Common suffixes (.com, .org, .gov)
Size ~0.3M params
Speed sub-millisecond
Output 150 numbers per token
💡
Think of it as a pattern-matching expert who can spot that "123-45-6789" looks like an SSN just from its shape — without needing to read the surrounding sentence.

Component 2: The Gating Fusion

This is our novel contribution — a smart switch that dynamically decides how much to trust each "eye" for each token.

How it decides

Character Signal
150 numbers
g
Gate (0 to 1)
Context Signal
384 numbers
output = g × characters + (1 − g) × context

When g is high (~0.9)

Trust the character eye more

● "123-45-6789" → SSN
● "john@email.com" → Email
● "4532-1234-5678-9012" → CC

When g is low (~0.1)

Trust the context eye more

● "Jordan Lee" → Person name
● "Acme Corporation" → Organization
● "742 Elm Street" → Address
Why this matters
The gate is learned, not programmed. The model figures out on its own when to rely on patterns vs. context. This is what makes it novel — no one has published this approach for PII.

Component 3: CRF Output Layer

The final "grammar checker" that ensures the model's output makes sense.

The BIO tagging system

Each token gets one of three labels for each PII type:

B-PERSON = Beginning of a person name
I-PERSON = Inside a person name
O = Outside (not PII)

Example

My → O
John → B-PERSON
Smith → I-PERSON
lives → O
at → O
742 → B-ADDRESS
Elm → I-ADDRESS

What the CRF does

Without CRF, the model might output impossible sequences. CRF prevents this:

✗ Without CRF (broken)

John → I-PERSON (can't start with I!)
Smith → B-SSN (SSN after PERSON?)

✓ With CRF (correct)

John → B-PERSON
Smith → I-PERSON
Speed overhead < 1ms
💡
CRF is like spell-check for entity labels. It knows the "grammar rules" (B must come before I, you can't jump from PERSON to SSN mid-entity) and corrects mistakes.

Why recall matters most for PII

In PII detection, missing a real SSN is far worse than flagging a random number. That's why we measure recall (what % of real PII did we catch?) more heavily than precision (what % of flagged items were actually PII?).

F2 / F5 Metrics

Standard F1 score weights precision and recall equally. F2 weights recall 2× more. F5 weights it 5× more. For critical PII, we use recall-heavy metrics.
